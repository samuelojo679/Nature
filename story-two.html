Into the heart of ancient forests

Old-growth forests hold time in their trunks, soil, and canopy layers.

FIELD NOTE

Ancient forests are living archives.

The oldest forests are not quiet; they are layered with motion, memory, and interdependence that reaches deep into the ground and upward to the highest branches.

In these ecosystems, every fallen tree feeds another cycle of life. Fungi, insects, birds, and mammals all participate in a network where decomposition is as important as growth. The forest does not merely contain life — it creates and sustains it in constant exchange.

These places often feel immense precisely because they are so intricate. The canopy intercepts sunlight, the understory filters air and moisture, and the soil preserves the slow work of millions of years. Each layer supports a distinct set of species, each one shaped by the one before it.

To walk beneath ancient trees is to enter a place where time is visible in growth rings, rooted trunks, and shifting shadows. It is a reminder that nature’s most profound systems are rarely dramatic — they are patient, layered, and enduring.
